# References

The sources behind the claims of `science.md`. Each one was checked on 2026-10-11 on the web, and each entry says exactly what
was read: the Crossref record (title, authors, year, journal, volume, pages, DOI), a PubMed record, a publisher page or a paper
that reports the figure. A reference that could not be checked was left out (Laplace on inverse probability and the Fagan
nomogram were not looked up and are not used). The slides cite them by author and year in a small line under the picture, and the
speaker notes name the key in square brackets. `packages/cli/test/science-refs.test.mjs` fails when a key in the notes is missing
here, or an entry here is never cited.

Format of an entry: `- [key] authors (year). Title. Venue. DOI. Claim: what the deck uses it for. Checked: what was read.`

- [bayes1763] Bayes, T. and Price, R. (1763). An essay towards solving a problem in the doctrine of chances. *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society of London* 53, 370-418. https://doi.org/10.1098/rstl.1763.0053. Claim: the rule of the "for the curious" slide; Bayes died before it was published and Price communicated it. Checked: the Crossref record (title, year, pages, DOI); the DOI redirects to the Royal Society's page of the essay.
- [kahneman1973] Kahneman, D. and Tversky, A. (1973). On the psychology of prediction. *Psychological Review* 80(4), 237-251. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0034747. Claim: people tend to ignore how common something is (the base rate) when they judge a case. Checked: the Crossref record; the claim is the paper's central argument, as summarised by a bibliographic abstract (representativeness, prior probabilities largely ignored).
- [casscells1978] Casscells, W., Schoenberger, A. and Graboys, T. B. (1978). Interpretation by physicians of clinical laboratory results. *New England Journal of Medicine* 299(18), 999-1001. https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM197811022991808. Claim: asked a question about a condition with prevalence 1 in 1,000 and a test with a 5 percent false positive rate, 11 of 60 students, house officers and physicians at Harvard teaching hospitals gave the right answer (about 2 percent), and 27 of 60 answered 95 percent. Checked: the Crossref and PubMed records (PMID 692627) for the citation. The article itself is not open: the counts (11 of 60, 27 of 60, the three groups of 20, the two parameters) are as reported in the full text of Manrai et al. (2014), below. The deck quotes only those counts.
- [manrai2014] Manrai, A. K., Bhatia, G., Strymish, J., Kohane, I. S. and Jain, S. H. (2014). Medicine's uncomfortable relationship with math: calculating positive predictive value. *JAMA Internal Medicine* 174(6), 991-993. https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2014.1059. Claim: it reports the Casscells counts above and a repeat of the question in 2013 in which 14 of 61 respondents answered correctly. Checked: the Crossref record and the PubMed Central full text (PMC4955674), read for the figures.
- [eddy1982] Eddy, D. M. (1982). Probabilistic reasoning in clinical medicine: problems and opportunities. In Kahneman, D., Slovic, P. and Tversky, A. (eds), *Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases*, Cambridge University Press, 249-267. https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511809477.019. Claim: physicians make major errors of probabilistic reasoning when they read a mammogram. Checked: the Cambridge Core page of the chapter (author, chapter 18, pages, abstract) and the Crossref record.
- [gigerenzer1995] Gigerenzer, G. and Hoffrage, U. (1995). How to improve Bayesian reasoning without instruction: frequency formats. *Psychological Review* 102(4), 684-704. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.102.4.684. Claim: stating a problem as counts of people ("10 out of 10,000") makes it easier to reason about than stating it as percentages. Checked: the Crossref record (title, authors, year, journal, volume, issue, pages, DOI). The claim is the paper's title and main finding; it was seen in search results and in later papers (Hoffrage and Gigerenzer 1998; Gigerenzer et al. 2007), not in the full text of the paper.
- [hoffrage1998] Hoffrage, U. and Gigerenzer, G. (1998). Using natural frequencies to improve diagnostic inferences. *Academic Medicine* 73(5), 538-540. https://doi.org/10.1097/00001888-199805000-00024. Claim: 48 physicians in Munich and Duesseldorf gave a correct answer in about 10 percent of the problems stated as probabilities and about 46 percent when stated as natural frequencies. Checked: the PubMed record (PMID 9609869) and its abstract.
- [gigerenzer2007] Gigerenzer, G., Gaissmaier, W., Kurz-Milcke, E., Schwartz, L. M. and Woloshin, S. (2007). Helping doctors and patients make sense of health statistics. *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 8(2), 53-96. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1539-6053.2008.00033.x. Claim: its abstract recommends natural frequencies instead of conditional probabilities to communicate risk to doctors and patients. Checked: the Crossref record and the PubMed record (PMID 26161749) and its abstract.
